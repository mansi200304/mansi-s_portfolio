 "There was a time when protecting a home meant locking its front door. Today, much of what we value lives behind screens, inside systems, and within lines of code. The doors have changed. So has the responsibility of protecting what lies behind them." 
 Origin Curiosity opened the door. Experience changed everything. 
 I didn't begin my journey knowing I wanted to become an engineer. 
 I started in finance, drawn to understanding complex systems, patterns, and the decisions that shape them. Then a roommate introduced me to ethical hacking. 
 What began as curiosity became something I couldn't stop thinking about. 
 How could a few lines of code expose an entire system? How could something designed to protect information become the very thing that puts it at risk? And how much could you learn about a system simply by understanding where it breaks? 
 I changed direction. From finance to cybersecurity. 
 At first, I wanted to understand how systems could be compromised. Then I experienced what that actually meant. 
 Ownership When the problem became personal. 
 My social media account was compromised. I lost access to years of personal data, memories, and information I never recovered. 
 Later, more than $1,000 disappeared after my payment card was skimmed at a restaurant. This time, I wasn't willing to leave the questions unanswered. 
 I investigated the suspicious transactions, examined the available evidence, identified a common network indicator, secured my accounts, and recovered the stolen money. 
 But what stayed with me wasn't the money. 
 It was the realization that behind every security incident is a system someone trusted, an assumption someone made, and often a vulnerability nobody noticed until something went wrong. 
 I learned that understanding a system is powerful. Understanding where it fails is even more important. 
 That experience changed the way I approached technology. I stopped seeing cybersecurity as simply finding vulnerabilities. I began seeing it as a responsibility to understand, investigate, and ultimately build better systems.

 Engineering I learned to investigate systems. Then I wanted to build them. 
 Cybersecurity taught me to ask what could go wrong. Software engineering gave me the tools to do something about it. 
 As a master's student in Computer Science at Arizona State University, specializing in cybersecurity, I've developed my engineering foundation through data structures, algorithms, object-oriented programming, and practical software implementations. 
 I work with Python, Java, JavaScript, and C/C++, moving between software development, automation, cloud systems, security tooling, and interactive applications. But what excites me isn't collecting programming languages. 
 It's taking something complicated, understanding its underlying structure, and turning it into something that actually works. 
 A geospatial application that makes complex data explorable. An automation pipeline that reduces repetitive investigation. A security tool that helps someone understand what happened inside a system. 
 Different problems. Different technologies. The same engineering instinct. 
 Understand the problem. Challenge the assumptions. Build the solution. Test where it breaks. Make it better.

 Philosophy The best systems are built by people willing to question them. 
 I believe good engineering requires two perspectives. 
 The builder asks: How can I make this work? The investigator asks: What happens when it doesn't? 
 I want to be both. 
 Because writing software is only part of the responsibility. Understanding how it behaves, how it scales, how it can fail, and how people depend on it matters just as much. 
 That's the mindset I bring to my research, my projects, and every unfamiliar problem I choose to explore. 
 I'm still learning. Still building. Still investigating. And that's exactly what makes this work exciting.
